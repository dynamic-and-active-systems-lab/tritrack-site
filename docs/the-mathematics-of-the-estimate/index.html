The mathematics of the estimate

This page is the full mathematics behind how the estimate is calculated, which explains the same method in pictures and says how often the 95% ellipse contains the animal. Start there if you have not read it. The colors are the same on both pages: a bearing is blue, the estimate red, the spread of the bearings κ magenta, the ellipse green and the likelihood gray. So are the reference numbers.

Coordinates and angles

The calculation works on a flat map in meters. TriTrack takes the average latitude and longitude of the places the selected bearings were taken from as the origin, and converts each place to meters east and north of it:

xi=R (λi−λ0)cos⁡φ0,yi=R (φi−φ0) x_i = R\,(\lambda_i - \lambda_0)\cos\varphi_0, \qquad y_i = R\,(\varphi_i - \varphi_0)

where (φi,λi)(\varphi_i, \lambda_i) is the latitude and longitude, in radians, of the place bearing ii was taken from; (φ0,λ0)(\varphi_0, \lambda_0) is the origin; and R=6 371 008.8R = 6\,371\,008.8 m is the earth’s mean radius. The finished estimate is converted back to latitude and longitude the same way.

Bearings are stored as compass directions from true north, already corrected for magnetic declination when they were recorded. The mathematics is simpler with angles measured counterclockwise from east, so each bearing βi\beta_i (in degrees) becomes

θi=90∘−βi. \textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} = 90^\circ - \beta_i .

The symbols used from here on:

  • nn is the number of selected bearings, numbered i=1,…,ni = 1, \dots, n.
  • (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i) is where bearing ii was taken, and θi\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} is its direction.
  • (x,y)(x, y) is a candidate position for the animal, and (x^,y^)(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x}, \textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y}) is the estimate. A hat always means an estimated value.
  • μi(x,y)\mu_i(x, y) is the direction from where bearing ii was taken to the point (x,y)(x, y), and did_i is the distance between them:
μi(x,y)=atan2⁡(y−yi,  x−xi),di=(x−xi)2+(y−yi)2. \mu_i(x, y) = \operatorname{atan2}(y - y_i,\; x - x_i), \qquad d_i = \sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2}.

The model and its likelihood

In words: the direction you read is the true direction to the animal plus an error. In symbols,

θi=μi(x,y)+εi, \textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} = \mu_i(x, y) + \varepsilon_i ,

where the errors εi\varepsilon_i are independent and follow a von Mises distribution centered on zero:

f(ε)=eκcos⁡ε2πI0(κ). f(\varepsilon) = \frac{e^{\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} \cos \varepsilon}}{2\pi I_0(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa})} .

Here κ≥0\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} \ge 0 is the concentration: the larger it is, the more tightly bearings cluster around the true direction. It plays the part of one over the variance. I0I_0 is a modified Bessel function, there only to make the curve integrate to one.

Multiplying the densities of all nn bearings and taking the logarithm gives the log-likelihood:

ℓ(x,y,κ)=κ∑i=1ncos⁡(θi−μi(x,y))  −  nln⁡(2πI0(κ)). \textcolor{#5c5c60}{\ell(x, y, \kappa)} = \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos\bigl(\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i(x, y)\bigr) \;-\; n \ln\bigl(2\pi I_0(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa})\bigr).

Only the first term depends on the position, and κ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} only scales it. So the best position is the one that makes ∑cos⁡(θi−μi)\textcolor{#5c5c60}{\sum \cos(\theta_i - \mu_i)} as large as possible: the point the bearings, taken together, point at most nearly. That is true whatever κ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} turns out to be, which is why the position can be found first and κ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} afterward.

Finding the position

There is no formula that gives the best position directly, so it is found by repeated improvement. In words: start from the point that best fits the bearing lines, then refit, giving each bearing a weight that depends on how far it is from the current estimate, and repeat until the answer stops changing. A far bearing gets less weight because the same angular error misses by more meters at a kilometer than at a hundred meters.

At the best position, the slope of the log-likelihood is zero in both directions:

∑i=1nsin⁡μidi sin⁡(θi−μi)=0,∑i=1ncos⁡μidi sin⁡(θi−μi)=0. \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\sin\mu_i}{d_i}\,\sin(\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i) = 0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\cos\mu_i}{d_i}\,\sin(\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i) = 0 .

Lenth’s trick (his Eq. 2.6 [1]) is to write these so they are straight-line equations in xx and yy once some terms are held fixed. With

si=sin⁡θi,ci=cos⁡θi,zi=sixi−ciyi, s_i = \sin\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i}, \quad c_i = \cos\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i}, \quad z_i = s_i x_i - c_i y_i,

and, evaluated at the current estimate (x^,y^)(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x}, \textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y}),

si∗=y^−yid^i 3,ci∗=x^−xid^i 3, s_i^{*} = \frac{\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y} - y_i}{\hat d_i^{\,3}}, \qquad c_i^{*} = \frac{\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x} - x_i}{\hat d_i^{\,3}},

the next estimate is the solution of

[∑si∗si−∑si∗ci∑ci∗si−∑ci∗ci][xy]=[∑si∗zi∑ci∗zi], \begin{bmatrix} \sum s_i^{*} s_i & -\sum s_i^{*} c_i \\[2pt] \sum c_i^{*} s_i & -\sum c_i^{*} c_i \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum s_i^{*} z_i \\[2pt] \sum c_i^{*} z_i \end{bmatrix},

with every sum running over i=1,…,ni = 1, \dots, n. (The quantity six−ciy−zis_i x - c_i y - z_i is the perpendicular distance from (x,y)(x, y) to the line of bearing ii, which is where the straight lines come from.)

The starting point is the same system with every si∗s_i^{*} replaced by sis_i and every ci∗c_i^{*} by cic_i: the point that minimizes the sum of squared perpendicular distances to the bearing lines.

A step that would make the fit worse is shortened. This is TriTrack’s addition to Lenth’s plain iteration (step 3). If the new estimate makes ∑cos⁡(θi−μi)\textcolor{#5c5c60}{\sum \cos(\theta_i - \mu_i)} smaller than at the current one, the app takes half the step instead, halving up to 30 times. Lenth’s own example never needs it, so its answer and step count are unchanged.

When it stops. TriTrack repeats the step until a whole step moves both coordinates by less than 0.1 mm, or until 200 steps have been taken, or until no part of a step improves the fit. In the last two cases the estimate is shown with the caution Solver did not fully converge — treat this estimate as provisional . If the system of equations cannot be solved at any step (for example, exactly parallel bearings), no estimate is made at all. The stopping rule is TriTrack’s own.

How spread out the bearings are

In words: once the position is found, each bearing’s residual (step 4) is how far, in angle, it misses the estimate. The closer the residuals are to zero, the larger the concentration κ^\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa}, and the smaller the ellipse.

The residual of bearing ii is ri=θi−μi(x^,y^)\textcolor{#c13b9e}{r_i} = \textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x}, \textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y}), taken the short way round the circle, so it lies between −180∘-180^\circ and 180∘180^\circ. Their average cosine is

Cˉ=1n∑i=1ncos⁡ri, \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos \textcolor{#c13b9e}{r_i} ,

which is 1 when every bearing passes exactly through the estimate and falls as they spread. The cosine is the circular measure of closeness to zero: for small angles 1−cos⁡r≈r2/21 - \cos r \approx r^2/2, so it acts as a squared error, and it is the quantity in the von Mises density. A(κ)A(\kappa) below is the average cosine that bearings with concentration κ show in the long run (the von Mises distribution’s mean resultant length). The maximum-likelihood κ^\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa} is the κ whose A(κ)A(\kappa) equals the measured Cˉ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C} (Lenth 1981, section 2) [1]:

A(κ^)=Cˉ,A(κ)=I1(κ)I0(κ). A(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa}) = \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C}, \qquad A(\kappa) = \frac{I_1(\kappa)}{I_0(\kappa)} .

Where TriTrack departs from the paper. This equation has no exact solution in closed form. Lenth gives an approximation (his Eq. 2.10); TriTrack instead uses the standard approximation from Fisher (1993) [5]:

κ^≈{2Cˉ+Cˉ3+56Cˉ5,Cˉ<0.53,−0.4+1.39 Cˉ+0.431−Cˉ,0.53≤Cˉ<0.85,1Cˉ3−4Cˉ2+3Cˉ,Cˉ≥0.85. \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa} \approx \begin{cases} 2\bar C + \bar C^{3} + \tfrac{5}{6}\bar C^{5}, & \bar C < 0.53,\\[4pt] -0.4 + 1.39\,\bar C + \dfrac{0.43}{1 - \bar C}, & 0.53 \le \bar C < 0.85,\\[8pt] \dfrac{1}{\bar C^{3} - 4\bar C^{2} + 3\bar C}, & \bar C \ge 0.85. \end{cases}

If Cˉ≤0\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C} \le 0, κ^\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa} is taken as 0, and if Cˉ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C} is exactly 1 it is infinite. In either case there is no ellipse.

One consequence worth knowing. The residuals are measured from a position that was chosen to make them small, and nothing corrects for that: taken together they are always smaller than the true errors. By choosing its two coordinates the estimate can cancel as much disagreement as two bearings carry, leaving n−2n - 2 bearings’ worth to measure the spread from (the two degrees of freedom White and Garrott say are lost, 1990, p. 69 [2]). With many bearings it makes little difference. With three to five it tends to make κ^\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa} too large and the ellipse too small; step 4 explains it, shows an example, and the coverage section measures the effect. With exactly two bearings that cross, both residuals are zero, κ^\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa} is infinite, and there is no ellipse.

The ellipse

In words: each bearing tells you a lot about the animal’s position across its line, and almost nothing along it. Bearings from different directions together pin the position down. A bearing tells you more the closer the animal is to where it was taken and the more concentrated the bearings are. Adding that up gives the uncertainty in each direction, and the ellipse draws it.

The information the bearings carry about (x,y)(x, y) is summarized in a two-by-two matrix (Lenth’s Eqs. 2.7 to 2.9 [1]), evaluated at the estimate:

I^xx=∑i=1nsi si∗,I^yy=∑i=1nci ci∗,I^xy=−12∑i=1n(si ci∗+ci si∗). \hat I_{xx} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i\, s_i^{*}, \qquad \hat I_{yy} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\, c_i^{*}, \qquad \hat I_{xy} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(s_i\, c_i^{*} + c_i\, s_i^{*}\bigr).

Since si∗≈sin⁡μi/di2s_i^{*} \approx \sin\mu_i / d_i^{2}, the term I^xx\hat I_{xx} is roughly ∑sin⁡2μi/di2\sum \sin^2\mu_i / d_i^2: a bearing pointing north or south says the most about the east-west coordinate, and less the farther away the animal is.

The covariance matrix of the estimate is the inverse of the information scaled by the concentration:

Q=(κ^ I^)−1=1κ^ (I^xxI^yy−I^xy2)[I^yy−I^xy−I^xyI^xx]. \textcolor{#1d7852}{Q} = \bigl(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa}\,\hat I\bigr)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa}\,\bigl(\hat I_{xx}\hat I_{yy} - \hat I_{xy}^{2}\bigr)} \begin{bmatrix} \hat I_{yy} & -\hat I_{xy} \\ -\hat I_{xy} & \hat I_{xx} \end{bmatrix}.

Its diagonal gives the standard errors and its off-diagonal the correlation:

SE⁡(x^)=Qxx,SE⁡(y^)=Qyy,ρ=QxySE⁡(x^) SE⁡(y^). \operatorname{SE}(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x}) = \sqrt{Q_{xx}}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y}) = \sqrt{Q_{yy}}, \qquad \rho = \frac{Q_{xy}}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat x)\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat y)} .

The ellipse’s size and direction come from the two eigenvalues of Q\textcolor{#1d7852}{Q},

λ1,2=Qxx+Qyy2±(Qxx−Qyy2)2+Qxy2, \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{Q_{xx} + Q_{yy}}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{Q_{xx} - Q_{yy}}{2}\Bigr)^{2} + Q_{xy}^{2}},

which give the semi-major and semi-minor axes (White and Garrott 1990, pp. 62–63 [2])

a=k λ1,b=k λ2,k=−2ln⁡(1−p), \textcolor{#1d7852}{a} = \sqrt{\textcolor{#1d7852}{k}\,\lambda_1}, \qquad \textcolor{#1d7852}{b} = \sqrt{\textcolor{#1d7852}{k}\,\lambda_2}, \qquad \textcolor{#1d7852}{k} = -2\ln(1 - p),

and the major axis points at the angle ψ=12atan2⁡(2Qxy, Qxx−Qyy)\psi = \tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}\bigl(2Q_{xy},\, Q_{xx} - Q_{yy}\bigr) counterclockwise from east, which the map draws as the compass direction 90∘−ψ90^\circ - \psi.

The confidence level. TriTrack sets p=0.95p = 0.95, so k=5.991\textcolor{#1d7852}{k} = 5.991 and each semi-axis is 2.448 standard deviations long in its own direction. k\textcolor{#1d7852}{k} is the 95th percentile of a chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom: if the estimate’s errors are normal with covariance Q\textcolor{#1d7852}{Q}, the squared distance from the estimate in standard deviations, dTQ−1d\mathbf{d}^{\mathsf T} \textcolor{#1d7852}{Q}^{-1} \mathbf{d}, is a sum of two squared standard normal variables, which is chi-square with two degrees of freedom. In one direction the same reasoning gives the familiar 1.96, since 1.962=3.841.96^2 = 3.84 is chi-square’s 95% point with one degree of freedom. This is the standard construction: White and Garrott build the 95% ellipse from Lenth’s covariance and the same chi-square value, relying on the estimate’s errors being approximately normal (1990, pp. 62–63; their Eq. 4.6 gives its area, π ∣Q∣1/2 χ22(0.95)\pi\,|\textcolor{#1d7852}{Q}|^{1/2}\,\chi^2_2(0.95)) [2]. It is the right size when κ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} is known, as it is in their construction, where κ comes from system testing. TriTrack estimates it from the bearings and does not widen the ellipse to allow for that (see how often the ellipse contains the animal). The axes and direction are what SAVE POSITION stores.

Why not just average where the bearings cross?

It is the obvious alternative, and Lenth’s paper explains why it is a poor one (his section 1) [1]. Three reasons are easy to see. With nn bearings there are n(n−1)/2n(n-1)/2 crossing points, but each bearing is in n−1n - 1 of them, so one bad bearing moves many points at once. Two nearly parallel bearings cross very far away, and that one point can drag the average a long way. And a pair of lines can cross behind where one of the bearings was taken, which says nothing about the animal at all. The maximum-likelihood estimate uses each bearing once and weights it by what it actually tells you. TriTrack does not show the crossing points.

When it goes wrong

The app checks each estimate and lists cautions in words. What each one measures:

  • Time span over six hours. The earliest and latest selected bearings are more than 360 minutes apart. Assumption 1 may not hold.
  • Near-parallel bearings. The bearings’ directions are almost the same. The test is the circular standard deviation of the compass bearings, −2ln⁡Rˉ\sqrt{-2\ln \bar R}, where Rˉ\bar R is the length of the average of their unit vectors; under 2∘2^\circ triggers it. Lines that nearly coincide cross at a poorly defined place.
  • Poor geometry. The 95% ellipse is more than ten times as long as it is wide, or reaches more than 5 km from the estimate along its long axis. This usually means every bearing came from one side of the animal (step 6).
  • Few bearings. The ellipse rests on five bearings or fewer, where the simulation found it contains the animal 70% of the time or less: Few bearings — the 95% ellipse usually understates the uncertainty. Add bearings if you can.
  • No ellipse. There is an estimate but no spread to measure, always with two bearings: No ellipse — two bearings can't measure their own spread. Add a third. With more than two it is rare, and the caution reads No ellipse — these bearings can't measure their own spread.
  • Observers at the same point. Every bearing was taken within 1 m of every other. No estimate is made.
  • Low-accuracy GPS. At least one bearing’s recorded position accuracy is worse than 20 m. The estimate is unchanged; the warning is that the ellipse does not include this error (assumption 4).
  • Did not converge. See finding the position.
  • One bearing, or a set that cannot be solved: the ray only, or nothing.

What no caution catches. A bearing that is wrong but agrees well enough with the others: a reflection, a misidentified frequency, a misread dial. It pulls the estimate and, by adding to the residuals, also enlarges the ellipse, but nothing on screen points at it. The next section shows how large that effect can be.

Validation against Lenth’s own example

Lenth’s paper works through an example: seven bearings toward a source whose true location is known, (7.5, 2.2) on his map grid (his Tables 1 and 2) [1]. A test in TriTrack’s code feeds those seven bearings to the same calculation the Position tab uses and checks the answer against the published one. It also adds the eighth bearing from the paper, taken at 250° from (5.6, 9.0), which is a signal reflected from the west. The test runs with the rest of the app’s test suite before every change to the code is committed.

The units are the paper’s map units, not meters. TriTrack’s values are what the calculation produces on those inputs today.

Why this is the case for unticking a doubtful bearing. One reflected bearing out of eight moved the estimate from 0.35 to 1.95 units from the true location, more than five times farther, and made the standard errors about ten times larger. The estimate did not fail and no caution fired: the ellipse simply grew to cover a wrong answer. On the Position tab, untick the bearing you doubt and watch the estimate. If it jumps and the ellipse shrinks, that bearing was disagreeing with all the others.

Where TriTrack differs from the paper

  • The concentration κ uses Fisher’s (1993) [5] approximation rather than Lenth’s Eq. 2.10 [1].
  • Only the maximum-likelihood estimator. Lenth’s paper also describes robust versions that give a stray bearing less weight. TriTrack does not implement them, which is why unticking matters.
  • A step that would make the fit worse is halved (step 3). Lenth’s iteration takes every step whole.
  • The stopping rule (0.1 mm, at most 200 steps) is TriTrack’s choice.
  • The flat map is a local projection centered on the observers, which is how TriTrack meets the paper’s flat-earth assumption on a real map.
  • The cautions and their thresholds (six hours, 2°, ten to one, 5 km, five bearings, 1 m, 20 m) are TriTrack’s own.

The 95% ellipse itself is not a difference: it is Lenth’s covariance scaled by chi-square, as White and Garrott (1990, pp. 62–63) [2] construct it.
