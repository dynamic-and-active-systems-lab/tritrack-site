How the estimate is calculated

The Position tab finds the place that best explains the bearings you selected and draws an ellipse around it labeled 95%. It uses Russell Lenth’s 1981 method [1], the standard one for radio-telemetry triangulation, and the 95% ellipse that White and Garrott (1990) [2] construct from it. This page explains it in pictures and says how far to trust the ellipse. The full equations, how each caution is measured, a check against Lenth’s own worked example and where TriTrack differs from the paper are on the mathematics of the estimate.

The idea in one paragraph

Each bearing is a line from where you stood toward the animal. Every bearing has some error, so the lines never all cross at one point. The method asks: for which position would this set of bearings be most likely? Taking each error as random, independent and usually small, it finds the position that makes the observed errors most plausible: a maximum-likelihood estimate. How much the bearings disagree then sets the size of the ellipse.

What the method assumes

The estimate is only as good as these assumptions.

  1. The animal did not move while the bearings were taken. The app warns when they span more than six hours.
  2. Each bearing points at the animal, plus a random error, with no systematic lean. A bounce off a ridge breaks this, and nothing in the calculation can tell (an example).
  3. Every bearing’s error has the same spread. So the plus-or-minus, quality and signal strength you record are drawn on the map and listed, but no bearing is trusted more than another because of them.
  4. Where you stood is known exactly. The app warns when the phone’s position was uncertain by more than 20 m, because that error is not in the ellipse.
  5. The study area is small enough to treat as flat, as Lenth [1] assumes. Over a few kilometers the error is far below a meter.

The method in pictures

Seven steps, each in words, a picture, its equation and what it means in the field. Pictures and equations share colors: a bearing is blue, the estimate red, the spread of the bearings (κ, kappa) magenta, the ellipse green, and gray shows how likely each place is; every picture also names them in words. The examples are simulated, so the animal’s true position is known (a star); the app never knows it.

1. One bearing is a range of likely directions, not a line

When you take a bearing of 270°, the animal is probably close to 270°, less probably at 260°, and very unlikely at 230°. So a bearing is better pictured as a fan of directions, darkest along the bearing and fading to either side. It says which way, not how far. How wide the fan is depends on how good your bearings are, and the method measures it with one number, κ: the larger κ, the narrower the fan.

The curve is the von Mises distribution, the circular equivalent of a bell curve. The chance of an error of ε\varepsilon between the bearing θ\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta} and the true direction μ\mu is

f(ε)=eκcos⁡ε2πI0(κ),ε=θ−μ, f(\varepsilon) = \frac{e^{\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} \cos \varepsilon}}{2\pi I_0(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa})}, \qquad \varepsilon = \textcolor{#306afc}{\theta} - \mu ,

where I0I_0 only makes the total chance add up to one. White and Garrott draw the same fans around three towers (1990, Figs. 4.11 and 4.12) [2].

In the field: κ is your bearing accuracy written another way. About ±5° is κ near 130, ±10° near 33, ±15° near 15.

2. Each bearing makes a ridge; together they make a hill

Spread each bearing’s fan out over the map and it becomes a ridge of likely places along its line. Multiply the ridges of all your bearings together and only the places that every bearing finds likely stay high: one hill. The top of the hill is the estimate.

Multiplying the chances and taking logarithms gives the log-likelihood, the hill’s height:

ℓ(x,y)=κ∑i=1ncos⁡(θi−μi(x,y))  −  nln⁡(2πI0(κ)), \textcolor{#5c5c60}{\ell(x, y)} = \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos\bigl(\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i(x, y)\bigr) \;-\; n \ln\bigl(2\pi I_0(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\kappa})\bigr),

where μi(x,y)\mu_i(x, y) is the direction from where bearing ii was taken to the place (x,y)(x, y). Only the sum depends on the place, and κ only stretches it, so the top is in the same place whatever κ is.

In the field: a bearing that disagrees with the others pulls the top toward its own ridge. That is why unticking a doubtful bearing can move the estimate a long way.

3. Finding the top of the hill

There is no formula for the top, so the app climbs to it. It starts where the bearing lines come closest to crossing, then takes steps by Lenth’s rule [1] until a step moves less than a tenth of a millimeter. A far bearing weighs less than a near one: the same angle misses by more meters at 1 km than at 100 m.

TriTrack adds one safeguard to Lenth’s rule [1]: a step that would land lower on the hill is cut in half, and again if need be. About 3% of simulated fixes need it.

How does the search know it has reached the top? At the top the hill is flat: its slope is zero both east-west and north-south. Those slopes are the derivatives of the hill’s height ℓ\textcolor{#5c5c60}{\ell} with respect to xx and yy, so the search stops where both derivatives are zero. Written out (with the common factor κ dropped), the two conditions are

∑i=1nsin⁡μidi sin⁡(θi−μi)=0,∑i=1ncos⁡μidi sin⁡(θi−μi)=0, \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\sin\mu_i}{d_i}\,\sin(\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i) = 0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\cos\mu_i}{d_i}\,\sin(\textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i) = 0 ,

with did_i the distance from where bearing ii was taken.

In the field: you never see this, but if the climb cannot settle the app says so with Solver did not fully converge — treat this estimate as provisional .

4. How spread out the bearings are: κ from the residuals

The app does not know your bearing accuracy; it works it out from the bearings. Once the top is found, it measures each bearing’s residual: the angle between the bearing and the line from where it was taken to the estimate. (It is the same word as in a regression: what is left once the fitted value is taken away.) Small residuals mean the bearings agree with each other, all passing close to one point: a large κ and a small ellipse.

The catch, and the main reason the ellipse can mislead. The residuals are measured from the estimate, and the estimate is the point that makes them smallest. The bearings’ true errors are measured from the animal, which is somewhere else. So, taken together, the residuals are always smaller than the true errors, in every fix; single residuals can be larger, as in the figure below. How much smaller depends on how many bearings there are.

Two bearings show why. Any two lines that are not parallel cross somewhere, so the estimate sits exactly on both, and both residuals are zero however far off each bearing was. Two bearings cannot show their own error at all, which is why the app draws no ellipse for them. A third bearing will usually miss that crossing, and only that miss shows. The estimate still uses all three bearings, but by choosing where it sits, east-west and north-south, it can always cancel as much disagreement as two bearings carry. So three bearings leave about one bearing’s worth of disagreement to measure the spread from, and ten leave about eight, which is why ten give residuals close to the true errors. White and Garrott make the same point for their goodness-of-fit test: 2 degrees of freedom are lost because the location is estimated from the same bearings (1990, p. 69) [2].

The residual of bearing ii is ri=θi−μi(x^,y^)\textcolor{#c13b9e}{r_i} = \textcolor{#306afc}{\theta_i} - \mu_i(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x}, \textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y}), where (x^,y^)(\textcolor{#da210c}{\hat x}, \textcolor{#da210c}{\hat y}) is the estimate (a hat always means a value estimated from the bearings). The app averages their cosines. The cosine is how circular statistics measures how close an angle is to zero: 1 at 0°, 0.996 at 5°, 0.966 at 15°, 0 at 90°, and the same for +10° as for −10° or 350°, which a plain average of angles would get wrong. For small angles 1−cos⁡r1 - \cos r is about r2/2r^2/2, so it works like a squared error. It is also the quantity in the von Mises formula of step 1, which is why the likelihood uses it.

Cˉ=1n∑i=1ncos⁡ri,A(κ^)=Cˉ, \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos \textcolor{#c13b9e}{r_i}, \qquad A(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa}) = \textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C},

Each κ comes with the average cosine its bearings would show in the long run: about 0.996 for κ = 132 (5° of scatter), 0.985 for κ = 33 and 0.966 for κ = 15. That expected value is A(κ)A(\kappa). The app takes the κ whose expected average cosine equals the one it measured, Cˉ\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\bar C}: the κ most likely to have produced these residuals (Lenth 1981, section 2) [1]. The formula it uses is on the mathematics page.

In the field: when three bearings cross in a small triangle, that does not show your bearings are accurate. The estimate can always sit right on two of the lines, so a small triangle can come from poor bearings, as it did in the figure above. Only more bearings can show how good they are.

5. From the hill’s shape to the ellipse

A steep, narrow hilltop gives a small ellipse; a broad one a large ellipse. The app fits an oval to the hilltop (the covariance Q\textcolor{#1d7852}{Q}, Lenth’s Eqs. 2.7 to 2.9 [1]). Drawn at one standard deviation, that oval is the inner, dashed ellipse in the second figure below. The app then enlarges it by 2.45 on each axis to make the 95% ellipse. That is White and Garrott’s construction (1990, pp. 62–63, Eq. 4.6) [2].

Why 2.45, and not the 1.96 of a bell curve? In one direction, 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations of its center. A position has two directions, east-west and north-south, and the animal has to be close enough in both at once. Taking the estimate’s errors as roughly normal, as the method does, the squared distance from the center, counted in standard deviations and added over the two directions, follows a chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom. 95% of that distribution lies below 5.991, so the 95% ellipse reaches 5.991=2.45\sqrt{5.991} = 2.45 standard deviations in every direction. The one-standard-deviation oval holds only 39%.

The ellipse’s semi-axes a\textcolor{#1d7852}{a} and b\textcolor{#1d7852}{b} (half its length and half its width) come from Q\textcolor{#1d7852}{Q} (White and Garrott 1990, pp. 62–63 [2]):

Q=(κ^ I^)−1,a=k λ1,b=k λ2,k=5.991, \textcolor{#1d7852}{Q} = \bigl(\textcolor{#c13b9e}{\hat\kappa}\,\hat I\bigr)^{-1}, \qquad \textcolor{#1d7852}{a} = \sqrt{\textcolor{#1d7852}{k}\,\lambda_1}, \quad \textcolor{#1d7852}{b} = \sqrt{\textcolor{#1d7852}{k}\,\lambda_2}, \quad \textcolor{#1d7852}{k} = 5.991 ,

where I^\hat I is what the bearings tell you about the position (next step), λ1\lambda_1 and λ2\lambda_2 (the eigenvalues of Q\textcolor{#1d7852}{Q}) are the oval’s squared semi-axes at one standard deviation, and k\textcolor{#1d7852}{k} is chi-square’s 95% point (5.991=2.45\sqrt{5.991} = 2.45).

In the field: the ellipse is only as large as κ says. If κ is too large, as it usually is with three to five bearings, the ellipse is too small.

6. Geometry matters

A bearing pins the animal down across its line and hardly at all along it. Bearings from well-spread directions pin it down every way; bearings all from one side leave it free along their common direction.

The ellipse’s shape comes from how sharply the hill curves at its top in each direction, which statisticians call the information, I^\hat I (Lenth’s Eqs. 2.8 and 2.9 [1]); step 5’s covariance is its inverse, scaled by κ. Each bearing adds to it: a lot across its own line, because a step sideways changes the angle that bearing would read, and nothing along it, because a step toward or away from where it was taken does not. It adds less the farther away the animal is, because a sideways step of so many meters changes the angle less at a distance. Approximately:

I^≈∑i=1n1di2[sin⁡2μi−sin⁡μicos⁡μi−sin⁡μicos⁡μicos⁡2μi]. \hat I \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{d_i^{2}} \begin{bmatrix} \sin^2\mu_i & -\sin\mu_i\cos\mu_i \\ -\sin\mu_i\cos\mu_i & \cos^2\mu_i \end{bmatrix}.

Bearings that all share one direction μ\mu add up to almost nothing in the other.

In the field: take the next bearing from across the ellipse’s long axis. The app cautions Poor geometry — try a different observer position when the ellipse is more than ten times longer than wide.

A 95% ellipse promises that 95 of every 100 contain the animal. A simulation can check, because it knows where the animal is. How often does the 95% ellipse contain the animal?

TriTrack uses the standard method: Lenth’s (1981) maximum-likelihood estimate [1] with White and Garrott’s (1990) chi-square 95% ellipse [2]. That method has a known limitation, and this section measures it so you can allow for it. Its 95% factor assumes κ is known in advance. When κ is estimated from the same few bearings, as TriTrack does, the ellipse comes out too small; the LOAS manual reports the same shortfall [3].

So the ellipse contains the animal less often than 95%, and much less with few bearings. What to do about it:

  • Take more bearings. In TriTrack’s simulation the 95% ellipse contained the animal about 40% of the time with three bearings, about 70% with five, and about 85% with ten.
  • With five or fewer, the app says so. Every estimate with an ellipse made from three, four or five bearings carries the caution Few bearings — the 95% ellipse usually understates the uncertainty. Add bearings if you can.
  • Read a three- to five-bearing ellipse as a minimum, not a boundary: the animal is often outside it.
  • Spread your stations around the animal (step 6), and untick a bearing you doubt and watch what changes: no ellipse can see a reflected signal.

Why it happens. The 95% factor (step 5) is exact only when κ is known. White and Garrott take κ from a bearing standard deviation measured beforehand by system testing (1990, Eq. 4.4, p. 59) [2]. TriTrack estimates κ from the same bearings, and with few of them it comes out too large (step 4), so the ellipse is too small.

Three sources, three measurements:

  1. The LOAS manual (Ecological Software Solutions; LOAS implements Lenth’s estimators): ellipses scaled by chi-square “will only represent a true accuracy of about 45-85% when the anticipated accuracy was set to 95%” (manual dated 1/1/2005, p. 11) [3].
  2. White and Garrott’s field test (1990, Table 4.1, p. 70 [2], from Garrott and others, 1986 [4]): three-tower fixes on transmitters at surveyed spots, where reflected signals were common. Maximum-likelihood ellipses under 0.6 ha contained the true location 41% of the time. Their κ was not estimated from each fix’s bearings; it was measured beforehand by system testing, so the few-bearings problem of step 4 does not explain this shortfall. The likely cause is bounce: reflected signals that make some bearings wrong by far more than κ allows. In the field both problems can happen at once, and their effects add up.
  3. TriTrack’s own simulation, below, of the app’s own calculation. For each number of bearings (3 to 10) and each scatter (5°, 10° or 15°) it made 2,000 fixes. In each fix the animal is at a known spot, and each station is placed at random on one side of it (anywhere in a half circle), 500 m to 2 km away. Each bearing is the true direction plus a random error drawn from a von Mises distribution (step 1). The scatter is that distribution’s circular standard deviation: about two bearings in three fall within ±scatter of the truth, as with an ordinary standard deviation. There is no bounce. The app’s calculation then makes the estimate and its 95% ellipse, and the simulation counts how often the ellipse contains the animal.

What TriTrack does not do (yet)

Published methods that would make the ellipse more trustworthy, recorded for a later version. Your export already holds what they need: every bearing, where it was taken, and any truth points.

  • System testing. Bearings toward transmitters at known spots measure your system’s bearing standard deviation, and so one κ for every fix (White and Garrott 1990, chapter 5, and Eq. 4.4, p. 59) [2]. With κ known the chi-square ellipse is the right size, and a compass bias shows up, which no single fix can reveal. LOAS accepts such a standard deviation (manual, p. 11) [3].
  • A goodness-of-fit test for bounce: with a known standard deviation, a fix’s squared residuals against chi-square on n−2n - 2 degrees of freedom flag bearings that disagree more than the system allows (White and Garrott 1990, pp. 68–69) [2].
  • A maximum ellipse size beyond which an estimate is not used: White and Garrott chose 0.6 ha for their system, and say each system needs its own test (1990, p. 72) [2].
  • The Andrews robust estimator (Lenth 1981, section 4) [1], which gives a stray bearing little weight. In White and Garrott’s bounce-prone test its ellipses under 0.6 ha contained the true location 78% of the time against 41% for maximum likelihood, though it gave no estimate for 154 of 400 locations (1990, pp. 70–71) [2].
  • F-based ellipses: LOAS’s F and Adjusted F scales, “usually providing a true accuracy of greater than 90%” (manual, p. 11) [3]. The manual gives no formula, so TriTrack cannot cite one yet.

For a methods section

A sentence you can adapt:

Animal locations were estimated from radio-telemetry bearings, corrected to true north, using the maximum-likelihood estimator of Lenth (1981) as implemented in TriTrack, which assumes independent von Mises bearing errors with a common concentration parameter estimated from each location’s bearing residuals. Uncertainty is reported as 95% confidence ellipses from the estimated covariance and the chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom (White and Garrott 1990).

Add the TriTrack version, how many bearings went into each location, and how you chose which to include. Because κ is estimated from each location’s own bearings, say that these ellipses contain the true location less often than 95% when few bearings are used (above).
